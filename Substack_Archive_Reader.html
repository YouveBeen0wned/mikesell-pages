The Modern Meditations

F.1

What would Marcus Aurelius write if he had a Twitter feed and a Six Sigma certification? This archive is the answer I am building. Not rules handed down from authority. Principles discovered through experience, tested against evidence, and revised when the evidence demands. Marcus kept a private journal that was not meant for publication. He was writing to himself, testing his own principles against his daily experience as emperor, soldier, philosopher. The Meditations is the record of a person in serious power trying to hold himself accountable to something greater than that power. The modern version of this practice looks different. The journal is a Thought Archive. The audience is the public. The accountability is no longer a private discipline. It is a relationship with the reader. The underlying project is the same. A person in the middle of building something, writing down what they believe, testing it against what they are doing, publishing the gap when they find one. The Michaelian philosophies are not a brand. They are notes from the field. From a specific person who has worked in healthcare systems, corporate environments, and community organizations. Who has watched what happens when principle meets pressure. Who has enough scar tissue to know the difference between a conviction and a performance. Hold me to these. When I fall short, say so. The accountability is the point of making them public. Principles are not statements. They are claims under examination. Public examination is what turns a claim into a working principle. The journal that no one reads is a record. The journal that is read is a contract.

"If anyone can show me that I am making a mistake, I will gladly change. It is the truth I am after, and the truth never harmed anyone."— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

What are your principles? Not your positions. Your principles. The ones that do not move when the argument is clever enough.

Unique Thoughts in a Copy-Paste World

F.2

Original thinking is not just rarer than it used to be, it is more valuable than it ever; and seemingly more difficult. I do not know if unique thoughts are valued anymore. That sentence is one I wrote years ago and have returned to many times since. Every time I return to it, I am more pessimistically confident that it's true. The information environment has been optimized for the rapid recirculation of familiar ideas packaged in familiar formats for familiar audiences. The algorithm rewards the content that consistently confirms. The influencer who challenges their own audience loses the audience. The politician who says something genuinely new loses the donors before they gain the followers. And yet unique thinking is the only source of genuine progress. Every useful idea that exists in the world today was, at the moment it was first articulated, unique. Often ridiculed. Sometimes suppressed. Eventually, if it was right, it's deemed inevitable. The person who thinks a thought no one has thought before is performing the essential function. They are extending the map of what is possible. The map that everyone else navigates by. I write because I have thoughts I have not found elsewhere. Whether they are valuable is not mine to assess. That is the reader's job. My job is to have them honestly and share openly. The copy-paste world does not need more copies. It needs the thing that cannot be copied: the unique thinking of a creative person sharing their vision.

"The man who follows the crowd will usually get no further than the crowd. The man who walks alone is likely to find himself in places no one has ever been."— Alan Ashley-Pitt, Author

Building Meaning From Fragmented Information

F.4

We live in a time in which it is very difficult to get mental traction. The signal-to-noise ratio has never been worse, making it so the ability to spot a narrative has never been more essential. The problem is not that there is too little information. The problem is that there is too much, arriving too fast, with too many competing claims on what it means. The person who cannot spot narratives amongst the fragmented inputs is not uninformed, they are overwhelmed. Overwhelmed minds default to the frameworks they were handed rather than building new ones - they see what they are trained to see. The consequence is not just personal. Oversimplifications harden into generalizations that dehumanize the people they describe and blaming "others" for what is wrong starts to seem rational. Spotting them is not a passive skill. It is an active practice that requires sustained effort and capacity. Being able to create a narrative is the discipline of taking disconnected data points and building a coherent account of what is actually happening. It requires the ability to sift through the noise while also placing events in historical context; the willingness to hold a provisional story loosely enough to update it when new information demands revision; statistical literacy, which allows you to evaluate the difference between a finding and a trend; and source evaluation, which allows you to weight the information you receive by the reliability of its origin; and transparent iteration that is shared frequently to keep all parties aware of changes. The political person who cannot do this work is permanently dependent on someone else's narrative. The narrative they receive was built by someone with specific interests.

"We don't see things as they are, we see them as we are."— Anaïs Nin

The Contra Code

F.5

If you ever played Contra, you know the code. Up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, start. Punch that in before the game starts and you got thirty lives instead of three. It set a very different tone for the game we were about to play. That code was a secret. Not a secret in the sense that it was locked away somewhere. It was right there in the game. But you were never going to find it by mashing buttons. The only way you ever learned it was because somebody who already knew it told you. That is what most secrets actually are. Not things that are hidden. Things that are only passed person to person, inside the circle, and never announced to everybody else. Every system has codes like that built into it. Not glitches. Not loopholes somebody stumbled onto. Codes put there on purpose by the people who built the system, that hand a real advantage to anybody who knows the inputs. The tax code is full of them. So is the legal system. So is the way you get a kid into the right school, or a project funded, or a permit pushed through. The advantage isn't that those people are smarter than you. It's that somebody let them in on it, and nobody let you in on it. Politics is the same game. There's the version they teach you in school. A bill gets introduced, it gets voted on, it becomes a law. Clean, simple, that's the surface. Then there's how it actually works. Committee chairs who decide what even gets a vote. Floor schedules. Amendments that gut a bill without killing it. Procedural moves with names nobody learns. The actual calendar math of getting something passed before a session runs out. Most people never see any of it. They vote, they watch the news, they argue about the stuff on the surface. And the folks who know the code count on that. They know which buttons move which people. They know what to do when the normal process gets jammed up. They know exactly how to turn what the public wants into law, and exactly how to stop it cold when somebody's paying them to. It is a secret, and that is exactly the problem. Not because you couldn't learn it. Because nobody with the advantage has any reason to tell you. So consider this me telling you.

"The most valuable commodity I know of is information."— Gordon Gekko, Wall Street

Immortality Through Good Works

F.6

The names that last are not the names of the powerful. They are the names of the useful. The person who built something that helped people outlives the person who accumulated something that served only themselves. Names like Washington, Lincoln, and King. Names like Curie, Darwin, and Tubman. What they share is not the accumulation of wealth or power, though some had both. What they share is that they built or revealed or fought for something that continued to matter after they were gone. The continuity does not run through the monument or the biography. It runs through the living impact on the people who came after. The child who learns to read in a school that exists because someone fought for public education. The patient treated by a medical protocol that exists because someone chose discovery over profit. The citizen who votes in an election made accessible because someone organized against disenfranchisement. The work persists. And the work is a form of presence, the person who did it continuing to affect a world they are no longer in. This is the only form of immortality I find credible. Not the continuation of consciousness somewhere else. The continuation of impact here. I think about that every time I decide how to spend the hours I have.

"The reward of a thing well done is to have done it."— Ralph Waldo Emerson

The Ripple Effect

F.7

When a drop of rain hits water, it makes a concentric ring that expands outward indefinitely. Every action we take enters the world the same way. It starts as a ripple. A small disturbance at the point of contact, expanding outward in all directions. As the ripple travels it can grow into a wave, gathering force from what it meets, the way a tidal wave is built from compounded ripples. Human action works the same way. That impact propagates in directions we cannot direct, through connections we cannot map, at timescales we will never observe. The ripple lands hardest on our direct circle, the people we actually touch with what we do. As it travels outward to broader audiences, the original signal weakens. But how each person receives it, interprets it, and passes it along creates a brand new ripple, originating from them, traveling onward through their own circle. One drop becomes a chain of drops, each making its own expanding ring. The consequences of what we do are never fully knowable. We cannot trace the full arc of what anything we do produces. The butterfly effect is not a metaphor. It is the actual structure of a system in which everything is connected to everything else. Nothing we do is truly small. The thing that seems insignificant at the point of contact may be the origin of a wave that matters enormously by the time it reaches its boundary. The kind word to the discouraged friend; the accurate piece of information shared with someone who needed it; the conversation you had with your neighbor that they carried into their next one; the vote cast for a candidate who wins by a single precinct. These are all drops hitting water, signaling the start of a storm.

"The butterfly effect: the sensitive dependence on initial conditions in which a small change in one state of a system can result in large differences in a later state."— Edward Lorenz, Meteorologist

The Authentic Pursuit of Mastery

F.8

What is an expert? Not the person with the most credentials but the person who has done the most intentional reps. We live in an age in which experts are disdained by entire segments of the population while simultaneously being manufactured at industrial scale by institutions that have learned to credential without enabling genuine expertise. Both ends of this spectrum are problems. The disdain is dangerous because some questions have right answers, and finding those answers require specific, developed capacity. The person who has spent twenty years studying infectious disease epidemiology knows things about pandemic dynamics that the person who spent twenty minutes reading about it on social media does not. The credential inflation is dangerous in the opposite direction. It produces experts by institutional decree, who hold authority without the substance the authority is supposed to represent. Genuine mastery is the product of honest repetition. Of doing the thing, failing, analyzing the failure, adjusting, doing it again. Not just experience. The specific kind of experience that produces learning: experience with honest feedback about whether what you did worked. Repetition alone is not enough. Ten thousand reps without the intent to improve produces consistency but not mastery. The reps that build expertise are the ones paired with attention to what each one is teaching and the willingness to change what is not working. The person who has done ten thousand reps that way is an expert in a way that no number of credentials replaces. The credential grants admission while the reps build capacity. Both can be earned but under pressure, only one of them shows up to do the real work.

"An expert is a person who has made all the mistakes that can be made in a very narrow field."— Niels Bohr, Physicist

Constitutional Obligation

F.9

The failure to advance the values of America and make us more perfect is not just a political failure. It is a constitutional one. The preamble of the United States Constitution includes a specific statement of purpose: to form a more perfect union. Not a perfect union. A more perfect one. The founders, whatever their contradictions, understood that the document they were writing was not the destination. It was the vehicle. The destination was the ongoing project of improvement toward the ideals the document itself defined. If the purpose of the constitutional project is to form a more perfect union, then the failure to pursue that improvement is not simply a missed opportunity. It is a departure from the stated mission of the enterprise. The policies that increase inequality rather than reduce it. The systems that restrict democratic participation rather than expanding it. The governance that serves narrow interests rather than the general welfare. These are not merely bad policies. They are specifically contrary to the constitutional project as stated in the document itself. This is not a partisan argument. It is a textual one. The text says we are supposed to be getting more perfect. The question is whether we are. These are the terms of the agreement we are all living under. More perfect is not an achievement. It is a direction. Get back on course.

"To reform means to shatter one form and to emerge into a greater."— Mary Antin

Wisdom, Knowledge, and the Need for Iteration

F.10

Knowledge is what you have. Wisdom is what you do with it. Most people stop at one or the other. The relationship between knowledge and wisdom is one of the oldest subjects in philosophy. Knowledge is the accumulation of accurate information. Wisdom is the capacity to apply that information appropriately in conditions of genuine complexity and uncertainty. The knowledgeable person can tell you the facts. The wise person can tell you what to do about them. Both are necessary. Neither substitutes for the other. The person with knowledge but not wisdom accumulates information without developing the judgment to use it well. They can produce an argument for almost any position because they have enough facts to populate any framework. But the argument is not the same as the right answer. The person with wisdom but not knowledge trusts their judgment in domains where their judgment has not been properly calibrated. They mistake the feeling of confidence for the substance of competence. The iteration between the two is what produces genuine expertise. You acquire knowledge. You apply it. You observe what happens. You update both your knowledge and your judgment based on what you observe. You apply again. Six Sigma is, at its core, a formalization of this iteration. Measure what is. Analyze why. Improve the system. Control the result. Then start again. That cycle does not end. The moment you think it does, you have stopped learning. Stopping learning is the beginning of becoming wrong without noticing.

"Any fool can know. The point is to understand."— Albert Einstein, Physicist

Words Are Architecture

F.11

Wisdom is the mastery of the magic of architecture and words. The spoken word is a force capable of shifting tones and altering the human psyche. The spoken word is a force. This is not a metaphor. The neuroscience of language is clear: the words we use to articulate our experiences shape the interpretations of our experiences and the way we imprint them; the words we use with our children shape the architecture that determines their capacity to interpret themselves and the world; and the words used in public discourse shape the frameworks through which we citizens evaluate our world. The person who controls language controls, to a significant extent, what is possible to think. This is why the manipulation of the spoken word is one of the most powerful forms of political power. The reframing of the estate tax as the death tax, torture as enhanced interrogation, corporate subsidies as investment incentives - each word choice is a structural decision that controls what is thought about the thing being named. Resistance to this manipulation requires the deliberate practice of precision, of calling things what they are. Refuse the framed euphemism and the intentional ambiguity that make the harmful thing sound neutral or their misleading messages unfalsifiable.

"The difference between the almost right word and the right word is the difference between the lightning bug and the lightning."— Mark Twain, Author

Our Tribalism Is the Result of Climate

F.12

Not the weather outside. The information climate. The ecosystem that determines what ideas can survive and spread. We did not choose tribalism. It was cultivated. Tribalism is not natural to humans in any simple sense. The evidence from anthropology and social psychology is complex on this. Humans do have evolved tendencies toward in-group preference, toward the differentiation of us and them. These tendencies served functions in specific historical conditions. But the degree of tribalism operative in current American political life is not simply the expression of those tendencies. It is the product of a specific information climate that has been engineered to produce it. The business model of social media is engagement. The emotions most reliably associated with high engagement are outrage, fear, and contempt. Outrage, fear, and contempt are most reliably produced by content about the threatening behavior of out-groups. The algorithm did not cause tribalism by accident. It cultivated it because tribalism is the most reliable producer of the emotional engagement that generates advertising revenue. The result is a population whose view of the opposing political tribe has been systematically distorted by years of exposure to the most extreme and threatening examples of that tribe, selected and amplified because they produced the most engagement. The tribalism is real. The people who feel it feel it genuinely. But the degree of it, the intensity and the specificity of it, is an artifact of the information climate they have been living inside. Changing the information climate changes the tribalism. Not overnight. Over time, with intention.

"We are born for cooperation, as are the feet, the hands, the eyelids, and the upper and lower jaws."— Marcus Aurelius, Emperor and Philosopher

Deciders vs. Doers

F.14

We need both. The work does not get done without doers. The direction does not get set without deciders. A system works only when the difference between those two roles is honored. Alan Watts told a story about a great farm worker who quit on the day he realized what the farm was actually for. The work had been meaningful when the work was the work. Digging post holes. Mending fences. The hands knew the task. The body knew the rhythm. Then the assignment got stretched. The post hole digger was reassigned to sort bad potatoes from good ones. The doing became deciding. Each potato required a judgment call. The certainty of execution was replaced by the ambiguity of evaluation. That is the day he quit. The story is not about a worker who got lazy. It is about a worker who got moved into a role that did not fit him. Deciders set strategy, allocate resources, and define the standards by which success will be measured. Doers build the thing. They produce the output the strategy is supposed to generate. The Unnatural American Economic Tree

C.8Revised

The economy is presented as a natural ecosystem. It is not. It is an inverted tree, top-heavy, structurally unsound, and grown in an unnatural way. A healthy, natural tree works by reciprocation. The roots pull water and minerals from the soil and send them upward. The leaves at the crown absorb sunlight and convert it into energy that flows back down through the whole tree. The trunk carries this exchange in both directions. Every part earns its place by giving something to every other part. The base is widest. The weight is managed. The structure holds. The American economic tree is inverted. The base, which is the working majority, the labor, and the public infrastructure, is narrow and underresourced. The trunk is strained. And at the top sits a concentration of wealth, asset ownership, and institutional power so disproportionate to the roots that hold it that the structure is inherently unstable. You do not need to be an arborist to know what happens when the weight above grows heavier than the roots and trunk can hold. It falls. It was grown this way on purpose. Someone chose the rootstock. Someone made the pruning decisions. Someone decided which branches would be fed and which would be starved. The tree serves the harvest its designers wanted, not the health of the roots that hold it up. The roots were never the point. The roots of the American economy are the labor of ordinary working people. The consumption of the majority. The public infrastructure funded by taxes. Worker Productivity vs. Wages

C.12

Worker productivity has grown dramatically. Wages have not kept pace. That workers stopped being paid for what they produce is one of the most important economic facts of the last fifty years. From roughly 1948 through the early 1970s, productivity and worker pay rose together. When workers produced more, they were paid more. The relationship was not perfect, but it was real enough to build the broad middle-class prosperity of the postwar period. Around 1979, the relationship broke. Productivity kept climbing. Pay did not keep up. Since then, productivity has grown about three and a half times faster than the pay of the typical worker. The value created by all that added productivity went somewhere. It went mostly to capital owners, through corporate profits and shareholder returns. If pay had simply kept pace with productivity, the typical worker would be earning substantially more per hour today than they actually do. This is not a natural market outcome. The doctrine that a corporation exists first to serve its shareholders redirected the surplus upward. The people who gained from them had real influence over the process that produced them. The good news is that what policy broke, policy can repair. Raising the minimum wage, protecting the right to organize, requiring profit-sharing, and redesigning the tax code are all tools that exist right now. The only missing ingredient is political will. That is the work.

For-Profit Nonprofits

C.13Revised

When the Fine Is Smaller Than the Profit

S.19

This is the clearest sign that the enforcement system is not a deterrent. It is a pricing structure for harmful behavior. Here is the most precise way to identify a broken enforcement system. If the fine for a violation is consistently smaller than the profit generated by that violation, the fine is not a penalty. It is a cost of doing business. The rational actor calculates: expected return from the violation, minus the expected fine multiplied by the probability of being caught, equals net profit from the violation. If that number is positive, the violation is financially rational. For large corporations engaging in systematic violations of financial regulations, environmental standards, consumer protection laws, and labor standards, the number is often very positive. The pharmaceutical company that knowingly marketed a drug for off-label uses and paid a settlement that represented a fraction of the revenue generated. The bank that systematically opened unauthorized customer accounts and paid fines smaller than the profits the fraud generated. The corporation that violated workplace safety standards repeatedly and paid fines smaller than the cost of compliance would have been. In each case, the regulatory fine was priced below the profit of the violation. In each case, the rational decision was to commit the violation and pay the fine. In each case, that is exactly what happened. The solution is obvious and politically blocked. Enforcement penalties need to be scaled to harm caused and profit generated, not to some fixed schedule set decades ago. Criminal prosecution needs to reach the decision-makers, not just the abstract corporate entity. Repeat violators need to face revocation of the operating licenses and regulatory approvals their business depends on. None of this is radical. All of it is available in the existing legal framework and has not been applied because the people with authority to apply it have donor relationships with the people they would be applying it to. The revolving door is the price list for impunity. We have to close it. When the fine exceeds the profit, the calculus changes. That is the whole point of enforcement. The next corporate fine you read about, look up the company's revenue for the relevant period. If the fine is less than one percent of it, the violation was priced. That is not justice. None of This Was Accidental

H.3Revised

The disparities are not the residue of history. They are the active, ongoing product of specific systems that continue to operate today. The racial wealth gap in America is not a relic. It is a result. It is the result of documented, traceable policy decisions made across generations that concentrated wealth among white Americans and actively extracted it from Black Americans. FHA redlining policies that denied mortgage access to Black families while subsidizing white suburban homeownership. The GI Bill's implementation, which provided transformational economic mobility to white veterans while Black veterans were largely excluded by local administration. The asset-stripping of prosperous Black communities through documented violence and legal dispossession. These were not failures of the system. They were the system operating as designed. The argument that these historical injustices are too distant to be relevant ignores the mathematics of intergenerational wealth transfer. The family prevented from building equity in 1950 did not pass equity to their children in 1980. Those children did not pass it to their children in 2010. The gap compounds. The current disparity is the compound interest of the original injustice. And the wealth gap is not the only gap. It compounds into a life gap. Black infants die at more than twice the rate of white infants, a rate that has not statistically improved in years. Black women die from pregnancy-related causes at more than three times the rate of white women. Chronic disease rates for heart disease, diabetes, stroke, and cancer land harder in Black communities, not because of biology but because of the accumulated stress, exposure, and reduced healthcare access that flow directly from the neighborhood and wealth conditions the earlier policies produced. The environmental burden lands in the same neighborhoods the redlining maps drew. EPA research finds Black Americans face a 54 percent higher health burden from air pollution than the population as a whole. Superfund sites, power plants, chemical facilities, and toxic waste storage cluster near communities of color. Water systems serving those same communities are significantly more likely to be contaminated. The children who grow up in these neighborhoods breathe more particulate matter, drink more contaminated water, and live closer to more toxic sites than the children whose grandparents were allowed to buy the suburban house. The criminal legal system produces the same pattern at steeper stakes. Black Americans are incarcerated in state prisons at six times the rate of white Americans. Black Americans are arrested for marijuana at nearly four times the rate of white Americans despite similar usage rates. Black people were killed by police at nearly three times the rate of white people in 2024, the deadliest year for police violence since national tracking began. Every one of those numbers is a policy outcome, not a natural fact. Political participation has been narrowed by the same design. After the Supreme Court gutted the Voting Rights Act in 2013, states began closing polling locations, tightening ID requirements, and restricting mail voting in ways that fall harder on Black voters. Gerrymandering carved concentrated Black voting power into diluted minorities. About one in every twenty-two voting-eligible Black adults is disenfranchised by a criminal conviction, compared to one in fifty-eight of the general population. Money is only the beginning. The real accumulation is in the things money buys that most people never think about.

The Stock Market Is Not the Economy

H.7Revised

This confusion is not innocent. It is one of the great misdirections in modern political communication.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up two hundred points today. This sentence is reported as economic news. It is actually financial news for a specific asset class owned disproportionately by a specific portion of the population.

The stock market is a mechanism for pricing the expected future profits of publicly traded companies. It is one part of the economy, and it is the part that most directly reflects the interests and conditions of people who own significant amounts of stock.

The top ten percent of households by wealth own approximately ninety percent of all stocks. When the market is up, the wealth of those households increases. The bottom fifty percent, who own roughly one percent of all stocks, experience essentially none of the direct financial impact of market movements.

Retirement accounts complicate the picture without changing the shape of it. A working household with a modest 401(k) does own stock, and does benefit when the market rises. But the scale is not comparable. A percentage gain on a five-thousand-dollar account is not a percentage gain on a five-million-dollar portfolio, and the rhetoric of a shareholder nation quietly conflates the two.

What most households experience is something the market is very bad at measuring: the price of food, the cost of rent, the availability of work at wages that cover actual expenses, the quality and accessibility of healthcare.

The political consequence of treating market performance as economic performance is that policy gets optimized for the metric. Low taxes on capital gains: good. High wages: bad. Loose monetary policy that inflates asset prices: good. Social spending that competes for resources: bad.

Fractional reserve banking is the system in which banks hold only a fraction of their depositors' funds in reserve and lend out the remainder.

Understanding how money works is the prerequisite to understanding why financial crises happen and who the policy responses to them serve.

The bank creates money when it lends. The money you borrow did not exist before you borrowed it.

AI Pivots. It Does Not Multitask.

V.11

What we call AI multitasking is actually rapid sequential pivoting between tasks. The difference matters more than most people realize — and closing that gap is the path to AGI. There is a common perception that the current generation of AI systems multitasks like a human being. This is not accurate. And the distinction matters enormously for understanding where AI is right now and where it is going. When a human multitasks — or attempts to — they are actually doing what AI systems do: rapidly switching attention between tasks, not processing multiple streams simultaneously. The difference is that humans carry context, emotion, and embodied judgment across those switches in ways that current AI systems do not. What current AI does is closer to pivoting. Ask it to write, and it writes. Ask it to analyze, and it analyzes. Ask it to code, and it codes. But it is not genuinely doing all three simultaneously. It is switching — quickly, fluidly, and with impressive surface coherence — between distinct processing modes. The responses feel integrated because the interface presents them that way. The underlying process is sequential. A truly multimodal AI — one that simultaneously processes visual, auditory, textual, and contextual information in an integrated stream the way a human does when they walk into a room and understand it all at once — does not yet exist at scale. The closest analogies are narrow domain systems. General integrated multimodality is still the frontier. This is the step that separates current large language models from what researchers call Artificial General Intelligence. AGI, if it is achievable, requires genuine simultaneous integration across modalities, not just fast pivoting between them. The system that can read your face, hear your tone, understand your words, and feel the context of the room — all at once, not in sequence — would be something categorically different from what we have now. We are not there. We are close enough to be astonished. Far enough away that the gap deserves honest acknowledgment. The political implications of this distinction are underappreciated. The AI systems shaping information environments, content moderation, and public discourse right now are powerful pivoting systems with significant biases embedded in their training data. The AI that would be genuinely transformative at governance scale — one that can hold the full complexity of a human situation simultaneously — is still being built. Understanding the difference changes how we regulate, how we deploy, and how we prepare. That conversation needs to happen before the system that closes the gap arrives.

The greatest democratic risk of the technological era is not AI replacing humans. It is technocrats using AI and networked infrastructure to accumulate a form of political power that no local government, no national legislature, and no international treaty body currently has the tools to constrain. The UI that makes those same tools available to everyone distributes power more broadly.
